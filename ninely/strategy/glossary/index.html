Sudoku glossary: every term explained

By Avi Israeli · Updated November 2026

How to use this glossary

Sudoku has a small vocabulary that grows quickly once you move past the basics. This page defines every term used in our guides and in the hints inside Ninely, in alphabetical order. Each technique entry links to a guide with a worked board. If you are new, start with how to solve a sudoku step by step and come back here when a word is unfamiliar.

Terms B to D

B

Bivalue cell
A cell with exactly two candidates. Bivalue cells are the raw material of naked pairs and of the Y-Wing, so they are worth marking when you scan a grid.
Box (block)
A 3 by 3 square of nine cells. The grid has nine boxes, numbered 1 to 9 from left to right and top to bottom, and each must contain every digit once.
Box-line reduction
A locked candidates pattern. If, within a row or column, a digit can only go inside one box, that digit must sit in that box's overlap with the line. So you remove it from the box's other cells. It is the mirror image of a pointing pair.

C

Candidate (pencil mark)
A digit that could still legally go in an empty cell, because it does not already appear in that cell's row, column or box. Writing candidates in the corner of a cell is called pencil marking. Most techniques work by deleting candidates until the answer is forced. See how to solve a sudoku.
Cell
One of the 81 squares of the grid. A cell holds a single digit from 1 to 9, or is empty while the puzzle is unsolved.
Column
A vertical line of nine cells. Columns are numbered 1 to 9 from left to right, and each must contain every digit once.

D

Difficulty grade
A label for how hard a puzzle is, based on the hardest technique a human-style solver needs to finish it, not on how many clues there are. A puzzle needing only singles is easier than one needing a naked pair, even if both look similar on the page.

Terms E to G

E

Elimination
Removing a candidate from a cell because a rule proves it cannot be the answer. Eliminations are the quiet half of solving: they place nothing, but they leave other cells with fewer options.

F

Fish
A family of patterns built on one digit across several lines. If a digit is limited to the same few columns in a set of rows, it must fill exactly those columns, so it can be removed from the rest of those columns. A size-two fish is an X-Wing, and a size-three fish is a Swordfish.
Full house
The last empty cell in a row, column or box. Only one digit is missing from that unit, so it goes there. It is the simplest placement in the game, and a special case of a naked single.

G

Given (clue)
A digit already printed in the puzzle at the start. Givens never change. The number of givens is the clue count, and fewer clues usually means a harder puzzle.
Guessing (backtracking)
Trying a digit with no logical proof, and undoing it if it leads to a contradiction. A well-made sudoku never needs it, because a logical path always exists. Solvers that guess are fast, but they teach you nothing, which is why our hints only use deductions.

Terms H to N

H

Hidden pair
Two digits that, inside one unit, appear as candidates in only the same two cells. Those two cells must hold those two digits, so every other candidate can be removed from them. See hidden pairs.
Hidden single
A digit that has only one possible cell left within a row, column or box, even though that cell may still show other candidates. The digit must go there. See hidden singles.
Hidden triple
The three-digit version of a hidden pair: three digits confined to the same three cells of a unit, so all other candidates can be cleared from those cells. See hidden triples.

L

Locked candidates
A name for two related patterns, the pointing pair and box-line reduction. In both, a digit is locked into the overlap of a box and a line, which lets you clear it from the rest of the other unit. See locked candidates.

N

Naked pair
Two cells in the same unit that each hold exactly the same two candidates. Those two digits must occupy those two cells, so they can be removed from every other cell in the unit. See naked pairs.
Naked single
A cell with only one candidate left. That digit is the answer. See naked singles.
Naked triple
Three cells in a unit whose candidates together use only three digits. Those digits are claimed by the three cells, so they can be removed elsewhere in the unit. The cells need not each hold all three digits. See naked triples.
Notation (RxCy)
A short way to name a cell: R for row and C for column, numbered from 1. R5C3 is the cell in row 5, column 3. Our guides and in-game hints use it so a cell can be named without a picture.

Terms P to S

P

Peer
Any cell that shares a row, column or box with a given cell. Every cell has 20 peers. A digit placed in a cell is removed from the candidates of all its peers.
Pivot and pincer
The parts of a Y-Wing. The pivot is the central cell with candidates AB. The two pincers are cells that see the pivot, holding AC and BC. Any cell that sees both pincers cannot be C.
Placement
Writing a digit into a cell, as opposed to an elimination. Every puzzle is finished by placements, but most of the thinking goes into eliminations that make them possible.
Pointing pair
Two or three cells in a box where a digit can go only along one row or column. That digit must sit in that line, so it can be removed from the line's cells outside the box. See locked candidates.

R

Row
A horizontal line of nine cells. Rows are numbered 1 to 9 from top to bottom, and each must contain every digit once.

S

Swordfish
A three-line fish. A digit is limited to the same three columns across three rows, so it must fill those columns within those rows, and can be removed from the rest of the three columns.
Symmetry
Many sudoku designs place clues in 180-degree rotational pairs: if a cell is a clue, so is the cell opposite it through the centre of the grid. Symmetry is purely cosmetic. It does not make a puzzle easier or harder.

Terms U to Y

U

Unique solution
The property that exactly one completed grid satisfies all the rules and agrees with the givens. A puzzle with two or more solutions is flawed, because logic alone could never choose between them.
Unit (house)
A row, a column or a box. Every cell belongs to three units, and every unit contains nine cells that must hold the digits 1 to 9 once each. Some authors say house instead of unit.

X

X-Wing
A two-line fish. A digit appears as a candidate in exactly two cells in each of two rows, and those cells line up in the same two columns. The digit must occupy opposite corners of the rectangle, so it can be removed from the rest of both columns.

Y

Y-Wing (XY-Wing)
A chain of three cells with two candidates each: a pivot with candidates AB and two pincers with AC and BC. Whichever way the pivot resolves, one pincer becomes C, so C can be removed from any cell that sees both pincers. See Y-Wing.
