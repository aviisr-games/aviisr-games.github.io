How to Find Naked Singles in Sudoku

By Avi Israeli · Reviewed by Ninely Engine Logic Council · Updated November 2026

What is a Naked Single?

A Naked Single (often called a Sole Candidate) occurs when a single empty cell can only contain one possible digit because the other eight digits (1 through 9) are already present in its peers—that is, in its row, its column, or its 3×3 box.

Unlike a Hidden Single (where a number can only go in one spot in an entire house), a Naked Single is identified by looking directly at the cell itself and ticking off all digits blocked by its intersecting neighbors.

Worked Example: Cell Row 5, Column 5

Consider the central cell at Row 5, Column 5 (R5C5) in the classic 30-clue puzzle below. Let us examine every intersecting unit that shares a peer relationship with R5C5:

  • Row 5 contains: 1, 3, 4, 8 (at R5C9, R5C6, R5C1, and R5C4)
  • Column 5 contains: 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9 (at R8C5, R6C5, R4C5, R1C5, R9C5, and R2C5)
  • Center 3×3 Box (Box 5) contains: 2, 3, 6, 8 (at R6C5, R5C6, R4C5, and R5C4)

Now check each candidate digit from 1 to 9 for R5C5:

1 (blocked by Row 5 & Col 5), 2 (blocked by Col 5 & Box 5), 3 (blocked by Row 5 & Box 5), 4 (blocked by Row 5), 6 (blocked by Col 5 & Box 5), 7 (blocked by Col 5), 8 (blocked by Row 5, Col 5 & Box 5), 9 (blocked by Col 5).

⚠️ The Tempting Wrong Move: Guessing 7 in R5C5

A player scanning only Row 5 (`4, 8, 3, 1`) might notice that `7` is missing from Row 5 and Box 5, and hurriedly place `7` in the center cell (R5C5).

Why this fails: Column 5 already contains a given `7` at the top (`R1C5`)! Placing `7` in R5C5 creates an immediate collision in Column 5. In Ninely, Smart Hints check all three intersecting units simultaneously so you never overlook a vertical blocker.

💡 Solving Tip: The "Rule of 8"

Whenever a row and column cross at a cell that shares a crowded 3×3 box, count the distinct numbers across all three units. If they cover 8 distinct digits, that intersection is guaranteed to be a Naked Single!

When to Look for Naked Singles

Naked singles do most of the work at the start and the end of a puzzle. Early on, a cell that sits where a crowded row, column and box meet often has only one digit left. Late in the solve, nearly every cell has few candidates, so naked singles appear one after another and finish the grid.

A good habit is to start with the most crowded houses. A row, column or box that already holds six or seven digits leaves only two or three empty cells, and each of those cells is easy to check against its other two units. Work outward from the fullest houses instead of scanning the grid cell by cell.

Common Mistakes

  • Checking only two of the three units. A digit can be blocked by the row, the column or the box. Skipping one of them is how a wrong placement slips in.
  • Trusting incomplete notes. If a pencil mark was never crossed off after a placement, a real naked single will look like a cell with two candidates. Update the notes after every digit you place.
  • Confusing it with a hidden single. A naked single is about one cell having one candidate. If the cell still has several candidates, the answer lies in a different technique.
  • Stopping after one find. Placing a digit removes that candidate from up to 20 peers, so the cells around it often become naked singles at once. Look at them straight away.

How It Connects to Other Techniques

Naked singles and hidden singles are two views of the same idea. One asks what a cell can hold, the other asks where a digit can go. When you run out of both, the next step is to remove candidates, which is what locked candidates and naked pairs do. Those eliminations usually leave a cell with a single candidate, so you end up back at a naked single.

Practice Routine

Take an easy puzzle and fill in every candidate for each empty cell. Then go through the grid and place only the cells with exactly one candidate. After each placement, update the notes of its row, column and box before moving on. Stop when no cell has a single candidate, and count how many digits you placed this way.
