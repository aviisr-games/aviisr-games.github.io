Designing Middle East 2030: one decision a month and a morning paper

Devlog · November 2026

Why an invented 2030

Middle East 2030 is a work of fiction. It is set in an invented 2030, and every event, headline and leader in it is made up for the game. We made that choice early and we have kept to it. A strategy game about a region is easy to read as commentary on the news, and we did not want to ask players to take a side in anything that is happening to real people. An invented timeline lets the game be about the shape of hard decisions instead of about who is right.

In practice this means the game borrows geography and political systems, not history. The capitals are real places on a map, and each has a recognisable way of governing, but the ministers, networks, newspapers and crises are invented. Nobody in the game is a real person, and no headline is taken from real life. If you notice something that feels familiar, it is because the pressures are familiar, such as an energy bill, a currency scare or a disputed stretch of sea, not because the game is reporting anything.

The monthly loop

The whole game is built on a small loop. You govern one month at a time. Each month, one decision is enough to settle it, and a second decision is optional. Then you tap once to print the morning paper, and the next front page tells you what your choices did.

We chose a newspaper as the main screen because it makes cause and effect easy to read. You act, and the paper answers in plain headlines and numbers. Three gauges keep you in office: Coalition, Economy and Stability. A choice that helps one of them often costs another, and the bill frequently arrives a month later. That delayed cost is the lesson we most wanted players to feel, and a paper that arrives the morning after is a natural way to deliver it.

The optional second decision exists for a reason. It lets a careful player push a little harder when they can afford it, and lets a nervous player keep a month quiet. A third decision is possible, but it costs political capital. We wanted extra action to be a real trade, not a free bonus.

A term is short, around six to eight months, so you can finish one in a sitting and try a different approach straight away. Losing is part of the design. The paper explains why a government fell, and that explanation is what makes the next attempt different.

A guided first term

The first time you play, the first term is guided. A short tour with a Next button and a step counter introduces four ideas: the gauges that keep you in office, how each option shows what it moves, the Print button, and the paper's line that says why something moved. The tour ends at your first paper, and a Skip guidance button is always available. After that the game leaves you alone, apart from a few one-time tips.

We kept the tour short because the loop is simple, and a long tutorial would suggest the game is harder than it is. If a crisis is still open when you try to print, the game asks first, and printing anyway needs a press and hold, because skipping a crisis has a price.

Eight capitals, five endings and a career

You choose one of eight capitals to govern from: Jerusalem, Cairo, Amman, Damascus, Tehran, Ankara, Riyadh and Abu Dhabi. All eight share the same core rules, but each has a different political system, different starting gauges and different pressures. Cairo, for example, is shaped by canal revenue, grain prices and the cost of disrupted shipping, while Amman is shaped by water and energy dependence and the need to balance protests at home against security agreements abroad. The same decision can work well from one chair and badly from another, which is why the game rewards trying more than one.

Every term ends with a special front page that records one of five outcomes: Stay in Office, Removed from Office, Strategic Embargo, Neighbor Reaches Threshold First, and Quiet Month Holds. The last of these is for a player who steers between a lopsided deal and a reckless strike until the clock runs out in calm.

Finished terms also build a career with five ranks, kept in your archive. Rising Leader comes from finishing a first term, won or lost. Negotiator comes from keeping a full term with a deal and no strike. Strategist comes from finishing a term in office in two different capitals. Veteran comes from finishing a hard opening in office or winning a second term. Statesman, the last rank, comes from earning the Statesman ending in certain capitals or a hard opening. We added ranks so that a lost term still counts for something, since the first rank is awarded whether you win or lose.

A three-month sample in your browser

Not everyone wants to install a game to find out whether it is for them, so there is a short briefing you can play in your browser. It is a three-month sample: three monthly dilemmas, an energy accord, a currency crisis and a maritime summit, each with two options. You start from the same three gauges, Coalition 65, Economy 60 and Stability 55, make three choices, and read a special front page at the end.

Two options across three months give eight possible paths, and each one ends with its own verdict. Signing the energy accord, for instance, lifts the economy and stability but costs coalition support, while rejecting it keeps the coalition happy at the price of the economy. Playing two or three runs shows the central idea of the full game in a few minutes: there is no choice that wins every gauge. You can play the briefing or read the chair guide, which lays out every path and each of the eight capitals.

Tone and the Everyone 10+ rating

Middle East 2030 is rated Everyone 10+ on Google Play, and that rating shapes how we write. Consequences are shown through newspaper headlines and numbers, never through graphic scenes. A government can fall, a trade route can close or a border can turn tense, and the game says so in the flat, slightly dry voice of a front page. We think that restraint suits the subject. It keeps the game readable for a younger player and keeps the focus on trade-offs, which is what the game is actually about.

We would rather the game be thoughtful than shocking. It is meant to be read and considered, and to leave a player with a better feel for how competing pressures pull on anyone who has to govern.
